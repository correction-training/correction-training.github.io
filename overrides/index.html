Correction Training for Diffusion Models

Donghoon Ahn1Runqian Wang1Alexei A. Efros1Taesung Park2Yash Belhe2

1 UC Berkeley 2 Reve

Paper PDFCodealphaXivarXiv Coming soon

TL;DR: We train a corrector that takes the diffusion model's prediction and refines it toward the same clean target. At inference, we apply correction multiple times at each denoising step. This simple framework significantly improves the sample quality of diffusion models, yielding results that are better than or competitive with classifier-free guidance.

Diffusion models often generate poor-quality images without guidance

Without guidance, diffusion models produce poor-quality samples even in recent large-scale models such as FLUX.2 Klein 9B. This has been a long-standing issue in diffusion-based image generation. Diffusion models generate good images only with classifier-free guidance.1

Early structural errors compound over later denoising steps

During training, the denoiser learns from noisy real images. During sampling, it receives intermediate states produced by its own imperfect predictions. This training–inference gap can let errors accumulate. In the example below, an unnatural mouth structure appears early; later denoising mainly adds detail without repairing it.

Method

How do we do this? The method is very simple.

Overall framework: We introduce a corrector trained to refine the base denoiser's prediction toward the same clean target used in the denoising loss (top row). During sampling, we iteratively correct the denoiser's prediction at each denoising step (bottom row).

Improve sample quality with iterative correction

Although the framework is simple, it's highly effective. Applying more corrections at each denoising step during inference produces significantly higher-quality images, with cleaner structures and fewer artifacts.

The corrected sampling trajectory starts from the same initial noise. Correction can change the coarse structure of a prediction before the sampler moves to the next noise level. Here, repeated refinement produces a coherent mouth and face.

Top: baseline trajectory, K=0. Bottom: one corrected trajectory, K=8. Each triplet shows the prediction before correction (k=0), after one correction (k=1), and after eight corrections (k=8) at a fixed noise level.

Left: SiT-REPA final samples at different K. Right: DiT FID and precision–recall, evaluated on 50K images per setting.

Correction significantly improves sample quality

In this work, we propose a method that enables diffusion models to generate high-quality, realistic samples without relying on guidance. We tested our method on four widely used baselines: DiT, SiT, SiT-REPA, and JiT. Our method significantly improves FID over the unguided baseline across all four models, using the same or a smaller inference budget than CFG.

Class-conditional ImageNet-256, FID on 50K samples, approximately 250 network evaluations per image (including denoiser and corrector calls for correction, and unconditional calls for CFG). We keep the number of network evaluations for correction no larger than that for CFG. See the paper for each model's settings.

Correction has better diversity and less oversaturation

If it's comparable to CFG, why don't we just use CFG? CFG has two well-known weaknesses: mode collapse and oversaturation.

  1. Mode collapse: CFG often substantially reduces the diversity of generated samples. This usually appears as boring or repetitive layouts, poses, and styles for a given text prompt.
  2. Oversaturation: CFG often makes sample colors highly saturated, contributing to the "AI look" of many text-to-image diffusion models.

Our correction avoids both issues in these examples. Below, we compare increasing the CFG scale with increasing the number of corrections:

SiT-REPA-XL/2, same class and initial noise, approximately matched NFE. Top: increasing CFG scale w. Bottom: increasing correction K without CFG.

Increasing CFG can push samples toward similar close-up portraits and oversaturated colors. Correction preserves more varied poses, framings, and backgrounds while producing sharp, coherent images.

Text-to-image results

Does our method work beyond ImageNet generation? Yes. We test it on NanoGen, a unified diffusion transformer implementation for ImageNet and text-to-image generation. The base model is pretrained on BLIP-3o's Short-Caption, Long-Caption, and JourneyDB subsets, whose original releases contain approximately 36M image–text pairs in total. Below, we show samples without guidance (top row) and with correction (bottom row). Correction significantly improves the samples, producing cleaner object structures.

Correction combined with CFG improves diversity and realism

We can combine CFG with correction to benefit from both. Adding correction can reduce the AI look, oversaturated or overcooked colors, and loss of diversity caused by CFG.

These text-to-image samples with CFG have a pronounced AI look and limited diversity: all the benches face forward.

Prompt: “a photo of a bench.” All four groups use the same eight seeds and 50 denoising steps. Columns: no CFG (w=1), CFG (w=6). Rows: no correction (K=0), correction (K=6).

Applying correction to the CFG prediction during denoising makes the samples more diverse, with more varied viewpoints, and more realistic.

Correction alone also generates high-quality samples with greater diversity and much less oversaturation; many now look like natural images. We can control diversity and prompt adherence by adjusting the CFG scale while continuing to apply correction during sampling. The corrected samples look better than the baseline samples both with and without CFG.

We also conduct a human study to assess whether people perceive improvements in sample quality and diversity with correction. This is practically important for researchers and developers building large-scale text-to-image diffusion models. Across 80 GenEval single-object prompts, we collected 800 responses per criterion (1,600 in total), with 10 judgments per prompt for each criterion, through Rapidata. The baseline uses CFG only, at DiffusionBench's default CFG scale.

Why does correction help?

Correction is simple, and it works across models. But why does it work so well? We look at two questions here; more analyses are in Section 4 (Discussion) of the paper.

The gain comes from applying correction iteratively

A natural suspicion is that correction simply adds parameters. To test this, we train two models from scratch with the same total number of transformer layers (LightningDiT, ImageNet 256×256, 80K updates): a 28-layer denoiser, and a 14-layer denoiser paired with a 14-layer corrector. With one correction per denoising step, the pair performs about the same as the deeper denoiser (FID 8.74 vs. 8.93).

The gain comes from repeating the correction. Applying the same corrector three times per step, with half as many denoising steps so that the compute budget is unchanged, lowers FID to 5.66. Correction is not merely a way to add parameters or inference compute.

LightningDiT on ImageNet 256×256, trained from scratch for 80K updates (675M vs. 681M parameters); FID on 8K samples, no guidance. Block width shows the number of transformer layers.

It narrows the training–inference gap

Surprisingly, correction does not lower the denoising error, the squared error used for training. After three or more corrections, the error is higher than the base denoiser’s. The catch is where this error is measured: on noisy training inputs, made from real images. During sampling, the denoiser instead sees states built from its own earlier predictions, as shown above, and what matters for generation is how well it handles those states. We hypothesize that correction helps by keeping them closer to what the denoiser saw during training.

To test this, we train a classifier to tell the two apart: is this noisy state from training, or from a sampling run? If it cannot do better than a coin flip, the two distributions are indistinguishable. For a standard SiT model, the classifier separates them easily, especially at low noise levels where errors have accumulated. One correction makes the task harder, and four corrections harder still. Correction narrows the training–inference gap, even though the error on training inputs goes up.

Held-out AUROC of a classifier trained on 100K intermediate states of SiT, per noise level (data from the paper’s Fig. on classifier two-sample tests; binary cross-entropy shows the same trend in the paper).
